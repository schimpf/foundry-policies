Layoff Runway Calculator: How Many Weeks Will Savings Last

After a layoff, one number matters more than most: how many weeks your money lasts. This page shows the formula, a worked example with real numbers, and a calculator you can use right here.

What "runway" means

Runway is the number of weeks you can cover your spending without a new paycheck. It uses three things:

  • Money available: savings plus severance you have not received yet.
  • Money coming in: weekly unemployment benefit, if you get one.
  • Money going out: your monthly spending, turned into a weekly figure.

The formula

The formula below is the one the Job Loss 12-Week Plan app uses.

  1. Available money = savings + severance still to come
  2. Spending per week = monthly spending × 12 ÷ 52
  3. Net cost per week = spending per week − weekly unemployment benefit
  4. Weeks left = available money ÷ net cost per week, rounded down to whole weeks

Two details matter:

  • Monthly to weekly uses × 12 ÷ 52, not ÷ 4. A year has about 4.33 weeks per month, so ÷ 4 makes your runway look shorter than it is.
  • If the benefit is equal to or higher than your weekly spending, the net cost is zero or less. Your money does not run down, so there is no end date. The app shows this as "Covered".

How severance is counted

Severance only counts for the part you have not been paid yet. The app assumes it is paid evenly from your layoff date to the date it ends:

  • Still to come = total severance × days left ÷ total days
  • With no end date, the whole amount counts as still to come.
  • Once the end date has passed, nothing is left to count.

Example: $6,000 of severance paid over 60 days. With 30 days left, $3,000 is still to come.

Worked example

Say you were laid off and have these numbers:

ItemAmount
Savings$12,000
Severance still to come$4,000
Weekly unemployment benefit$400
Monthly spending$3,500
Of that, cuttable (streaming, dining out, subscriptions)$600

Step 1. Available money: $12,000 + $4,000 = $16,000.

Step 2. Spending per week: $3,500 × 12 ÷ 52 = $807.69.

Step 3. Net cost per week: $807.69 − $400 = $407.69.

Step 4. Weeks left: $16,000 ÷ $407.69 = 39.2, so 39 weeks.

Now cut the $600 of cuttable spending:

  1. New monthly spending: $3,500 − $600 = $2,900
  2. Spending per week: $2,900 × 12 ÷ 52 = $669.23
  3. Net cost per week: $669.23 − $400 = $269.23
  4. Weeks left: $16,000 ÷ $269.23 = 59.4, so 59 weeks

Cutting $600 a month adds 20 weeks:

Why the benefit assumption matters

The formula assumes your unemployment benefit continues for the whole runway. It does not. The U.S. Department of Labor reports that most states pay benefits for up to 26 weeks, and a few pay fewer. Check your state's rules on the Department of Labor's unemployment insurance pages (dol.gov).

To test the worst case, set the weekly benefit to $0. In the example above, that gives:

  • Net cost per week: $807.69
  • Weeks left: $16,000 ÷ $807.69 = 19.8, so 19 weeks

Run both versions. The truth is usually between them.

How to get your inputs right

  1. Savings: count only money you can reach quickly. Leave out retirement accounts. Early withdrawals can trigger taxes and penalties.
  2. Severance: use the amount after tax if you can. If you only know the gross amount, your result will be a little high.
  3. Benefit: use the weekly amount your state tells you, not the maximum.
  4. Monthly spending: add up rent or mortgage, food, utilities, insurance, debt payments and everything else you pay each month. Health insurance may cost much more after your employer stops paying its share.
  5. Cuttable spending: only count costs you could stop within a month.

Using the numbers

  • Under 12 weeks: cut spending first and file for unemployment now if you have not yet.
  • 12 to 26 weeks: you have time, but start applications early. Job searches often take months.
  • Over 26 weeks: the benefit is not the main risk. Watch your spending and recheck monthly.

These ranges are rules of thumb, not advice. This page gives general information for the US. It is not legal or financial advice.

Bristol Stool Chart: Types 1 to 7 Explained for IBS

The seven Bristol stool types in a table, what each can mean, and how doctors use the chart to sort IBS into constipation, diarrhea or mixed.

The Bristol stool chart sorts stool into seven types by shape and consistency. People with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) use it because "loose" or "hard" means different things to different people. A type number is a shared, exact word you can give your doctor.

The seven types

TypeShapeWhat it usually points to
1Separate hard lumps, like nutsSlow transit, constipation
2Sausage-shaped but lumpySlow transit, constipation
3Like a sausage but with cracks on the surfaceNormal range, slightly firm
4Like a sausage or snake, smooth and softNormal range
5Soft blobs with clear-cut edgesLeaning loose, faster transit
6Fluffy pieces with ragged edges, mushyLoose, diarrhea
7Watery, no solid pieces, entirely liquidDiarrhea

The scale was published by Lewis and Heaton in 1997 ("Stool form scale as a useful guide to intestinal transit time", Scandinavian Journal of Gastroenterology, 32(9):920-924). Their finding: the harder and lumpier the stool, the longer it stayed in the gut. The softer and more liquid, the faster it moved through.

How to read the chart

  • Types 1 and 2 are hard stool. They suggest constipation.
  • Types 3 and 4 are generally seen as the normal range. Type 4 is the one most often called ideal.
  • Types 5 to 7 get progressively looser. Types 6 and 7 suggest diarrhea.

One odd bowel movement means little. The pattern over days and weeks is what counts. Many people with IBS see a mix, for example type 1 on one day and type 6 two days later.

How the chart is used for IBS

The Rome IV criteria are the current diagnostic standard for IBS. They use the Bristol chart to name the IBS subtype (Lacy et al., "Bowel Disorders", Gastroenterology 2016;150(6):1393-1407).

SubtypeRule
IBS with constipation (IBS-C)More than 25% of bowel movements are type 1 or 2, and less than 25% are type 6 or 7
IBS with diarrhea (IBS-D)More than 25% of bowel movements are type 6 or 7, and less than 25% are type 1 or 2
IBS mixed (IBS-M)More than 25% are type 1 or 2, and more than 25% are type 6 or 7

These rules are for sorting a diagnosis your doctor has already made or is considering. They do not diagnose anything by themselves. Blood in the stool, weight loss without trying, fever or waking at night with symptoms need a doctor's attention, whatever the type.

Worked example: counting your own percentages

Say you logged 20 bowel movements over two weeks:

  • Type 1 or 2: 7 movements
  • Type 3 or 4: 8 movements
  • Type 5: 2 movements
  • Type 6 or 7: 3 movements
  1. Type 1 or 2: 7 ÷ 20 = 35%. That is above 25%.
  2. Type 6 or 7: 3 ÷ 20 = 15%. That is below 25%.
  3. This pattern fits the constipation-leaning rule (IBS-C). Your doctor makes the call.

Counting by hand over weeks is tedious, and paper logs get lost. That is where a diary helps.

Tips for using the chart well

  1. Pick the closest type. If a stool sits between two types, choose the nearer one and be consistent.
  2. Log each movement. Type, urgency and whether you felt empty afterward all matter.
  3. Note blood or mucus. Flag it each time and tell your doctor.
  4. Add the time. Timing against meals and symptoms shows patterns.
  5. Keep it up for at least two weeks. Short logs mislead.

## The formulas

All shapes work the same way: surface area times average depth gives cubic feet or cubic meters, and a conversion gives you gallons or liters.

1. Average depth = (shallow depth + deep depth) / 2.
2. Surface area depends on the shape (see below).
3. Volume = area x average depth.
4. Convert to gallons or liters.

### Area by shape

- **Rectangle:** length x width.
- **Round:** pi x (diameter / 2) x (diameter / 2).
- **Oval:** pi x (length / 2) x (width / 2).
- **Kidney:** 0.9 x length x average width. This is the common pool industry formula, (A + B) x 0.45 x length, where A and B are the widths of the two ends. The calculator uses one average width.
- **Freeform:** measure or estimate the surface area yourself, then use it directly.

### Conversions

- 1 cubic foot = 7.4805 US gallons.
- 1 cubic meter = 1,000 liters.
- 1 US gallon = 3.7854 liters.
- 1 foot = 0.3048 meters.

## Worked examples

### Rectangle

A pool is 32 ft long and 16 ft wide. It is 3 ft deep at the shallow end and 5.5 ft at the deep end.

1. Average depth = (3 + 5.5) / 2 = 4.25 ft.
2. Area = 32 x 16 = 512 sq ft.
3. Volume = 512 x 4.25 = 2,176 cu ft.
4. Gallons = 2,176 x 7.4805 = about 16,278 gallons.

### Round

An above-ground pool is 20 ft across and a steady 4 ft deep.

1. Area = 3.1416 x 10 x 10 = 314.16 sq ft.
2. Volume = 314.16 x 4 = 1,256.6 cu ft.
3. Gallons = 1,256.6 x 7.4805 = about 9,400 gallons.

### Oval

An oval pool is 30 ft by 15 ft and 4 ft deep.

1. Area = 3.1416 x 15 x 7.5 = 353.4 sq ft.
2. Volume = 353.4 x 4 = 1,413.7 cu ft.
3. Gallons = 1,413.7 x 7.4805 = about 10,575 gallons.

## How to measure

- Measure the water surface. Ignore the outside of the pool wall.
- For depth, measure from the waterline to the floor. Do not start at the coping.
- For a sloped floor, measure the shallow end and the deep end. The calculator averages them. If the floor has a steep drop in the middle, split the pool into two parts and add the results.
- For a freeform pool, split the shape into rectangles and circles, work out each area, and add them up.
- A spa or hot tub is often listed on the manufacturer's data sheet. Use that if you have it.

## How accurate is it?

Formulas assume straight walls and a floor that slopes evenly. Real pools have steps, benches and curved floors. Expect an error of about 5 to 10%. For dosing, a good estimate is enough, because you retest after each addition anyway.

If you know how many gallons a hose adds per hour, or you read a water meter while filling, compare that with the result.

## Why it matters

- **Chlorine:** the number of ounces to raise FC by 1 ppm scales directly with volume.
- **Acid and baking soda:** doses for pH and total alkalinity scale the same way.
- **Stabilizer and calcium:** these are slow to correct, so an error in volume can cost you weeks.

Doses are estimates. Retest before you add more, never mix chemicals, wear protection, and follow the product label.

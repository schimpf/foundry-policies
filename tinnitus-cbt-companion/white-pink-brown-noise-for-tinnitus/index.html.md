## White, pink and brown noise at a glance

White, pink and brown noise are three kinds of steady background sound. They differ in how much energy sits in the low pitches compared with the high pitches. That difference decides how each one sounds, and it is the main reason people prefer one over another when they use sound with tinnitus.

| Noise | Energy by pitch | Drop per octave | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| White | Equal at every frequency | 0 dB | Bright hiss, like a radio between stations |
| Pink | Equal in every octave | 3 dB | Softer hiss, like steady rain or wind |
| Brown | Strongly weighted to low pitches | 6 dB | Deep rumble, like a distant waterfall or a jet engine heard from inside |

An octave is a doubling of pitch. In pink noise, each octave up is 3 dB quieter, so the high end is less sharp than in white noise. In brown noise, each octave up is 6 dB quieter, so the sound is dominated by bass.

## Which one fits which tinnitus

There is no rule that says one color works for everyone. These are common starting points:

- **High-pitched tinnitus (hissing, ringing, whistling):** white noise covers the same range, so it hides the tone most. It can feel harsh at higher volume.
- **Tinnitus that you find sharp or tiring:** pink noise has the same hiss with less treble.
- **Low humming or a tinnitus that is worse at night:** brown noise is gentle and works well at low volume in a quiet bedroom.

Try each one for a few minutes on the same day. Pick the one you would not mind hearing for an hour.

## A simple way to test the three

1. Sit somewhere quiet and note your tinnitus on a 0 to 10 scale for loudness and for annoyance.
2. Play one noise at a low volume. The noise should be clearly audible, but it should not cause discomfort.
3. After 5 minutes, rate annoyance again.
4. Repeat with the other two noises, with a short break in between.
5. Choose the noise with the lowest annoyance score, and the one you find easiest to ignore.

Do the same test on two or three different days. Tinnitus changes from day to day, so one session can mislead you.

## Volume matters more than color

Keep the volume comfortable. Loud sound over long periods can damage hearing, and it can make tinnitus more noticeable afterward. If the noise is so loud that you cannot hear your own surroundings, turn it down.

## What the research says about sound and CBT

The clinical practice guideline *Tinnitus*, published by the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation in 2014 (Tunkel et al., *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery*, 151(2 Suppl)), says clinicians may recommend sound therapy. It lists it as an option. The same guideline recommends cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for people with persistent, bothersome tinnitus.
See a doctor before you rely on any self-help tool if your tinnitus is new, changed suddenly, is in one ear only, beats in time with your pulse, or comes with hearing loss, dizziness or pain.

## How Tinnitus CBT Companion makes these sounds

Tinnitus CBT Companion generates all three noises on your phone, so they work without a connection.

- **White noise:** random values at every sample, so every frequency has equal energy.
- **Pink noise:** white noise run through a filter that lowers the level by 3 dB per octave (the Paul Kellet filter).
- **Brown noise:** white noise run through a leaky integrator, which lowers the level by 6 dB per octave.
